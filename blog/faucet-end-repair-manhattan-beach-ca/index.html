Faucet End Repair in Manhattan Beach, CA: Aerators, Cartridges, and Drips

The end of a faucet is the aerator, the spout tip, and the seal behind it. Most drips, sprays, and weak streams start there.

Start with the cheap fixes, work toward the cartridge, and stop when the faucet body itself is damaged.

By the Manhattan Beach 24/7 Plumbing team. Updated October 15, 2026.

Where problems start

What faucet end repair covers

Faucet end repair means fixing what happens at the spout tip and the parts that control flow to it: the aerator screen, the cartridge or valve seat inside the handle, and the O-rings and washers between them. It does not mean replacing the faucet. Most end problems are small parts that cost a few dollars.

The signs are easy to read. Water sprays sideways, the stream is thin, the faucet drips after you shut it off, or the handle leaks around its base. Each symptom points to a different part, and the order of repair matters, because fixing the wrong part first just adds a second problem.

For anything beyond the basic fixes, our faucet and sink repair service covers cartridges, valve bodies, and replacements, with a clear price before work starts.

Clearing an aerator in ten minutes

The aerator is the small screen at the spout tip that mixes air into the stream. It collects grit and mineral flakes.

  1. 1

    Remove it

    Wrap the spout tip in a cloth to protect the finish and turn the aerator counterclockwise by hand. If it is stuck, use pliers with the cloth in between. Some spouts use a small key, which comes with the faucet.

  2. 2

    Look at what came out

    Disassemble the screen, washer, and housing. White crust is scale. Dark grit is sediment from the line. Metal flakes can mean a failing supply line.

  3. 3

    Soak and brush

    Put the pieces in white vinegar for an hour, then scrub with an old toothbrush. Rinse. Replace the rubber washer if it is flat or cracked.

  4. 4

    Flush the line, then refit

    Before reinstalling, run the faucet for 30 seconds to push debris out of the spout. Then thread the aerator back on and snug it by hand. If flow is still weak, the cartridge or the supply line is next.

Cartridges, spouts, and handles

If the aerator is clean and the faucet still misbehaves, the issue is inside the body.

Cartridge replacement

Single-handle faucets use a cartridge that moves up and down and left and right to mix. Close the shutoffs under the sink, remove the handle, pull the retaining clip or nut, and lift out the cartridge. Match it exactly to the new one, then reassemble and test for drips.

Spout end and O-ring leaks

Swivel spouts seal with O-rings at the base. When those harden, water seeps down the side. Replace both rings, coat them lightly with plumber's grease, and keep the spout moving smoothly. A leak that persists after new O-rings usually means a worn body.

Handle drips

Water running down the handle points to a failed packing nut or O-ring under it. Tighten the packing a quarter turn, or replace it. Do not overtighten, because the handle then binds.

Sink versus shower

A kitchen or bath sink faucet has accessible shutoffs under the cabinet. A shower valve usually sits behind the wall with no stop of its own, so you may need to close the main or a fixture stop. If the repair means cutting into the wall, call a plumber.

Hard water and the faucet end

Mineral scale is the number one cause of clogged aerators and stuck cartridges. The City of Manhattan Beach's 2023 Annual Water Quality Report lists the hardness at two sources, and your tap sees a blend.

Scale shows up first at the hot water side and at the aerator, where evaporation leaves mineral behind. In practice, expect to clean aerators often, and expect cartridges to stiffen sooner. Vinegar soaks help, but if you descale often, a water softener or filtration setup cuts the cause for every fixture.

Stop and call a plumber if you see these

Faucet work should stay simple. These signs move the job out of DIY.

  • The shutoff will not close.

    A seized or leaking stop valve means water keeps running while you work. Do not force it.

  • The faucet body is cracked or pitted.

    Corrosion at the base or deck means replacement, not repair.

  • Hot and cold mix when the faucet is off.

    Cross-flow through worn check stops affects the whole line.

  • Water stains appear under or beside the cabinet.

    A slow leak can soften the cabinet floor. A leak detection visit can trace it.

  • Low pressure everywhere.

    That points to the supply or the pressure regulator, not the faucet end.
