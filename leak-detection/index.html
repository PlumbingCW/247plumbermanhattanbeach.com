Leak detection

What leak detection actually finds

A leak detection visit answers one question: where is the water going? Supply pipes in a house stay under pressure all day, typically somewhere between 40 and 80 psi, so even a pinhole keeps pushing water out long after anyone has noticed.

Most of those leaks sit behind drywall, under flooring, inside a slab or in the buried line between the meter and the house. Detection uses instruments to narrow that down to a spot you can mark with a pencil, so the repair opens a small patch instead of a whole room.

We run this as an emergency service because a leak you cannot see tends to get worse in the dark. Call the 24/7 dispatch line any hour, and ask about same-day appointments when available.

When a hidden leak cannot wait until Monday

Some leaks are a nuisance and some are an emergency. If you can hear water running behind a wall, see a ceiling stain growing, or find warm or soaking floor near a bathroom, treat it as urgent, because water that stays in framing or insulation starts to damage material within a day or two.

Our dispatch line is open 24/7. Whether the stain showed up on a Friday night or the meter is spinning on a holiday, tell the dispatcher where the water is showing and we will book the earliest visit we can, with same-day appointments when available.

While you wait for the plumber

  1. Close the main shutoff valve if water is visibly spreading. In many homes it is on the street side of the house near the hose bib, or at the meter box.
  2. Switch off power to any room with wet ceilings, soaked walls or standing water, and keep outlets in that room unplugged.
  3. Move rugs, boxes and furniture off wet floors and photograph every damp area with the date visible.
  4. Write down the meter reading and the time before you close the valve. It helps us confirm the leak is on the supply side.

Why does my water bill keep climbing?

A bill that rises for two or three cycles in a row, with no new guests, pool or lawn, is one of the most common reasons people call us. Supply leaks are quiet. They do not flood anything. They just add a few gallons an hour that nobody notices until the statement arrives.

The cheapest check costs nothing and takes about twenty minutes. It tells you whether the water is leaking at all, and roughly whether the leak is inside the house or on the line outside. Do it before you pay anyone.

The water meter test, step by step

Your water meter is the one honest witness on the property. When no fixture is using water, it should sit still. Here is how to read it.

  1. 1

    Shut every fixture off

    Close all taps, make sure dishwasher and washing machine are idle, pause the ice maker and any irrigation timer, and check that nobody flushes. A softener recharging or a hot tub refilling will fool the test.

  2. 2

    Find the low-flow indicator

    Lift the meter box lid. Most meters carry a small triangle, star or dial that spins when even a trickle moves through. Note the position or the last digits on the register, and the time.

  3. 3

    Watch and wait

    If the indicator is turning with everything off, water is leaking somewhere downstream of the meter. If it is still, wait fifteen to thirty minutes and read it again. A moved register with no use is a leak, even if the dial looked still at first.

  4. 4

    Split house from yard

    Close the main shutoff at the house and check the meter again. If it stops, the leak is inside the house or in the pipe past the valve. If it keeps moving, the leak is between the meter and the shutoff, which means the buried line.

Meters usually read in cubic feet, and one hundred cubic feet equals about 748 gallons, which is why a small billing change can hide a big volume. Toilets are the most frequent false alarm, so drop a few drops of food coloring in each tank and see if color reaches the bowl within fifteen minutes. If it does, the flapper is leaking, a smaller job covered by our toilet repair crews.

Other signs that water is escaping

The meter is only one clue. These are the physical signs that a leak has already started to show itself.

  • Sound of running water at night

    Hissing or a faint rushing noise in a wall or floor, with all fixtures off, usually means a pressurized line is leaking.

  • Warm spots on the floor

    A warm patch on tile or hardwood points at a hot water line under the floor. It is also one of the first signs of a slab leak.

  • Musty smell or peeling paint

    Moisture inside walls grows mold behind baseboards and bubbles paint long before water drips.

  • Low pressure at one end of the house

    If the shower weakens but the kitchen tap is fine, a leak on that branch may be stealing flow before it reaches the fixture.

  • Soft or sunken ground outside

    Green patches, soggy soil or a depression along the path from the meter often follow a buried line break.

  • Cracks or lifted flooring

    Water under laminate or wood makes boards cup and seams open. Grout in a shower or tile floor that keeps going dark can mean a leak below it.

How we find a leak without demolition

No single instrument finds every leak. We pick tools based on pipe material, depth, floor covering and what the meter test told us, and we usually cross-check with a second method before anything is cut.

Acoustic listening

A leaking pipe under pressure makes a hiss that travels through metal pipe and soil. We use an acoustic ground microphone and contact sensors on fittings, then move the pickup in a grid. The loudest spot over the pipe marks the leak. It works best on metal pipe and is weaker on plastic, which damps sound.

Thermal imaging

A thermal camera shows temperature differences on a wall, ceiling or floor surface. A hot water leak under a slab shows as a warm bloom. A moisture meter then confirms whether the area is wet. It will not see through thick material on its own, so it is a clue, not a final answer.

Tracer-gas testing

For stubborn leaks, we drain the suspect line, fill it with a safe mix of about 5 percent hydrogen and 95 percent nitrogen, and sweep the surface with a sensitive sniffer. Hydrogen is the smallest molecule, so it rises through concrete, tile and soil and shows up above the leak. It is slower to set up but excellent for plastic lines.

Pressure testing

We isolate the system with test plugs and watch a gauge. If pressure drops with no flow, there is a leak. Then we isolate branch by branch, hot side against cold side, until the drop stops. This narrows the leak to a section before the other tools pinpoint it.

Manhattan Beach homes

Hill lots, beach lots and why leaks travel

Water follows gravity and the path of least resistance. On a steep Hill Section lot, a leaking line can send water downhill under floors and along footings, so the stain you see may be far from the source. That is why we trace from the meter and the shutoff outward, not from the stain inward.

Closer to the ocean, in the Sand Section and El Porto, ground tends to be sandy and drains quickly. A buried leak there can soak away without a wet patch ever reaching the surface, which makes the meter test and acoustic listening more important than visual signs.

The City of Manhattan Beach supplies the water here. For where the meter ends and your responsibility begins on a specific property, ask your water utility before any digging.

Landlords and managers

Leak detection for rentals and multifamily buildings

Rental owners meet leaks differently. The tenant reports a drip or a stain, but the water might originate in the unit above, in a shared riser or in the yard. Chasing the wrong unit costs days and a lot of drywall.

In a duplex or triplex, we shut off one unit's supply at a time and watch the meter, which separates the building into sections quickly. Where the building has a master meter and individual units, we test each branch with pressure gauges. Units with a shared wall are checked with acoustic sensors on both sides.

What property managers and owners should have ready

Meter access and shutoffs

Know where each unit's isolation valve and the building's main are, and keep lockbox or gate access current so a late-night call does not stall.

Tenant notice

Give tenants notice of a visit and ask them to leave sinks and showers off. Moisture photos from the tenant speed up the first call.

Records

Past bills, prior leak repairs and any earlier moisture reports help us connect old problems to the new one and sort the owner's repair from a tenant fixture issue.

Commercial buildings, restaurants and offices have their own risks, and our team handles those through commercial plumbing when the property is not residential.

After the leak is found

Finding the leak is half of the job. We mark the location on the surface, explain what we found and what the repair will involve, and give you a clear price before work starts. You are not obligated to let us do the repair, though many owners do because the crew already knows where to open.

Typical repairs are a spot fix on a corroded section, a replaced fitting, or a reroute around the damaged length. When the problem is deep under a floor, we go through your options on slab leak repair, and when the line under the yard has failed we cover water line repair. If pipes are failing in several places, ask about repiping.

Permit rules differ by job. Ask the Manhattan Beach Community Development department what applies before work begins on pipe replacement.

What drives the cost of leak detection

Pricing is not a flat number because no two leaks are the same. These are the factors that decide what you pay.

How many methods are needed

A leak found by the meter test and one acoustic sweep takes less time than one that needs tracer gas and a second pass.

Pipe material and depth

Metal pipe carries sound well. Plastic and deep burial need more equipment and time.

Access to the area

Furnished rooms, locked units and tight crawlspaces slow the survey.

Size of the property

A single bathroom is a short job. A fourplex or a whole building takes systematic testing floor by floor.

Repair after the find

Detection is one line item. Opening a wall, replacing pipe and restoring the surface are separate and itemized.

Insurance often covers sudden damage from a burst or accidental discharge, but may not cover a slow leak or ordinary wear. Ask your insurer before you decide, and keep our written findings, which many carriers ask for.

It also notes that lead and copper at the tap come mainly from corrosion of household plumbing, and that the City cannot control the pipes inside your home.
