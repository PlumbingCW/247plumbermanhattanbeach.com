Plumbing installation

What plumbing installation means for a project

Plumbing installation covers three layers of work. The rough-in goes inside walls and under floors: supply pipes, drain lines, vents and the stub-outs for each fixture. The trim stage sets the visible pieces such as sinks, toilets, faucets and showers. Between them sits testing, where the system is pressurized and checked for leaks before anything is closed up.

Good installs are decided early. Where the drain runs, how far the vent sits from the trap, and which fixture ends up at the far end of the supply line all affect pressure, noise and how often something clogs. We plan these on paper first, then confirm on site against the framing.

New fixtures and runs are installed to the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) published by IAPMO, and larger jobs may need inspection, so ask the building department first.

What we install

Most projects are a mix of these. Each one can also be done alone.

Fixtures

Sinks, toilets, tubs, shower valves, hose bibs, washing machine boxes and ice maker lines. We set them to the fixture's specification sheet.

Supply runs

Hot and cold lines from the main or a tee, sized for the number of fixtures, with insulation on the hot side where it passes through cold spaces.

Drain and vent runs

Gravity drains with the right fall, typically a quarter inch per foot on smaller lines, plus vents that let the trap breathe.

Shutoff valves

A quarter-turn ball valve at each fixture, and a whole-house valve you can find in the dark.

Gas and appliance lines

Gas piping for ranges and dryers is a specialized task. We confirm the scope before quoting.

Pressure control

A pressure regulator and an expansion tank where the system needs them, to protect fixtures and fittings.

PEX, copper or something else for the supply side

Pipe choice is a trade-off, and most new residential work today uses PEX. This is how the common options compare.

MaterialStrengthsLimits
PEXFlexible, fewer joints, resists freezing splits, quick to run through finished wallsCannot sit in sunlight, needs proper crimp or expansion fittings
CopperLong track record, rigid, handles heat and sunlightHigher material cost, soldered joints, can corrode in aggressive water
CPVCCheap and simple to joinBecomes brittle with age and heat, so it is a less common choice for new work
Galvanized steelFound in old buildings onlyCorrodes from the inside, restricts flow, and is usually removed

Which material suits your job also depends on local code and the water chemistry. If you are replacing old pipes throughout the house, the question moves to repiping.

How an installation job unfolds

Scheduled work follows an order. Skipping a stage is how leaks end up inside walls.

  1. 1

    Scope and plan

    We walk the space, count fixtures, locate the nearest drain and supply, and note any wall or floor changes needed. You get the price before work starts, with the fixture list itemized.

  2. 2

    Rough-in

    With the walls or floor open, we run supply lines, drains and vents, set stub-outs at the right height, and secure everything to framing.

  3. 3

    Pressure and drain test

    Supply lines are pressurized and watched on a gauge. Drains are filled with water or tested with air. Any inspection the building department requires happens here, before the walls close.

  4. 4

    Trim and final check

    Fixtures are set, valves labeled where useful, and every connection checked under full flow. We show you where each shutoff is.

Remodels and ADUs

Remodels, additions and accessory dwelling units

A bathroom remodel that keeps the toilet and tub where they are is a different job from one that moves them. Moving a drain across floor joists means notching or boring framing, which has limits, and a slab floor means cutting concrete to reach the line. We tell you early if a layout you drew will cost more than the one next to it.

An ADU or a garage conversion needs its own supply, a drain that ties into the existing sewer lateral at the right point, and a water meter or shutoff plan that fits your property. Because the sewer lateral in Manhattan Beach belongs to the owner, the extra flow from a new unit is worth discussing with the City first.

Sizing the supply matters in a house that is gaining a bathroom. If the existing service line is small, a new shower at the end of a long run can leave the kitchen with a trickle. We check pressure and flow before we promise a layout.

Manhattan Beach note

Permits, licensing and lot types in Manhattan Beach

Permit rules vary by scope, so ask the Manhattan Beach Community Development / building department before you start. We will describe the work in writing so you can ask them the right question, and we make the site available when an inspector needs to see it.

Check the license of anyone you hire. California requires a contractor license for projects of $1,000 or more in labor and materials, and the Contractors State License Board (cslb.ca.gov) lets you verify one. The CSLB advises paying no more than 10 percent down or $1,000, whichever is less, with a few exceptions.

Lots differ across town. On the steep Hill Section, the slope matters when you plan a drain, because the fixture level relative to the street and the sewer lateral decides how the line must run. Flatter beach-side lots raise other questions, and salt air is worth considering for any exposed fixture, valve or hose bib.

What decides the price of an installation

Scheduled work can be priced in detail before it begins. Here are the pieces.

Scope and fixture count

Each fixture adds supply, drain and vent work as well as the fixture itself.

Open or finished walls

Running pipe through exposed studs is much faster than fishing it through plaster and tile.

Materials

PEX, copper and the fixtures you choose all come at different prices.

Floor type and layout changes

Slab cutting and moving a drain across joists raise the labor.

Inspections and scheduling

Time for inspections and coordination with other trades is part of the bid.

Insurance does not usually pay for planned upgrades. If a new install is part of a loss from burst pipe damage, ask the insurer before work starts.
