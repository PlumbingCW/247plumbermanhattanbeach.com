Planned work

Is it time to replace the pipes?

Pipes do not announce their retirement. They hint. A pinhole leak here, rust-colored water there, a shower that has gradually weakened. Any one of these can be fixed alone. When they pile up, the economics change, because each spot repair costs a visit, opens a wall and leaves the rest of the line unchanged.

That is the point where repiping becomes the sensible conversation. We walk you through what the symptoms mean, what a repipe involves and whether a partial replacement would do the job.

A repipe is governed by the California Plumbing Code, based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), which sets which pipe materials, sizes and supports are allowed for potable water.

Three symptoms that point to the pipes themselves

We start from what you notice at the tap, because that is how most repipe decisions begin.

  • Discolored water

    Brown, yellow or orange water, especially first thing in the morning, often means iron rust from galvanized pipe. Blue-green stains around fixtures point to copper corrosion. Run the cold tap for a minute: if it clears, the pipe is shedding, not the supply.

  • Low pressure that never recovers

    Galvanized steel scales on the inside until the opening is a fraction of its original size. Aerators and fixtures may be fine while the pipe behind them is choking.

  • Pinhole leaks

    A damp spot, a stain on the ceiling or a bill that creeps up, followed by another pinhole a few feet away. Pitting along the length of copper means one repair will not hold.

If you are unsure whether the problem is a single leak or a failing system, our leak detection team can confirm the source before you commit to replacing anything.

PEX or copper: the real trade-offs

Both materials are accepted for residential supply in California where the local code permits them. Which is better depends on the house, the water and your preferences.

Local code and the building department decide what is accepted on your project. Ask the Manhattan Beach Community Development department what applies, and we will follow it.

What a whole-home repipe involves

The work is organized so the house is usable as much as possible, and it follows a set order.

  1. 1

    Walk-through and route plan

    We look at where water enters, the water heater, each bathroom, the kitchen and the laundry. We decide the manifold or trunk layout and mark where walls and ceilings will need access.

  2. 2

    Shutoff and rough-in

    We shut the water off, drain the old system and set the new main run. We then run new lines to each room through the walls, attic or crawlspace, one zone at a time.

  3. 3

    Connect fixtures and test

    Each fixture is connected, and the system is pressure tested before any wall is closed. A pressure regulator and expansion tank are checked or replaced as part of the set-up.

  4. 4

    Close up and restore

    We cap off the old lines, close access holes and flush the system. Patching drywall, texture and paint may be a separate trade, so we spell out who does what.

Sequencing and wall access

The order matters because water is off while pipes are connected. We plan to restore water to bathrooms and the kitchen as each zone is finished, so you are not without water for the entire job. Plan on some shutoff periods and a temporary arrangement for a day or two, such as a nearby toilet or bottled water.

Wall access is the part people worry about. PEX can often be fed through small holes cut at the top and bottom of a wall. Copper needs more openings because rigid pipe cannot turn corners. Tile, plaster and finished stone add cost, so we try to route through closets, attics, crawlspaces and the garage. If your home has a second floor, we plan the vertical runs together.

When partial is enough

Repiping one bathroom or one line

Not every case needs the whole house. If the damage is limited to a bathroom, a kitchen run or the line from the heater, a partial replacement may be the efficient answer. We say so plainly when the rest of the pipe looks sound.

If the pipe is failing under a concrete floor, the alternative to breaking the slab is a reroute through walls or the attic. Our slab leak repair page lays out those options. If the failing pipe is the one outside, that is a separate job covered under water line repair.

What drives the price of a repipe

House size and bathrooms

More fixtures and more floors mean more pipe and more connections.

Material

PEX and copper differ in material cost and labor time.

Wall and ceiling access

Finished surfaces, tile and plaster take more care to open and repair.

Water heater and fixture work

Many owners replace shutoff valves, angle stops, the regulator and expansion tank at the same time.

Patching and finishing

Drywall, texture and paint are often scoped separately. Ask what is included.

Insurance usually does not pay for a planned repipe due to age or corrosion, but may help with the water damage from a sudden burst. Ask your insurer and keep records of any leaks.

Manhattan Beach note

What the City's water report says about your plumbing

The City's 2023 Annual Water Quality Report reports a blend of local groundwater and treated imported water. Groundwater hardness averaged 392 mg/L as calcium carbonate, which is roughly 22.9 grains per gallon, while imported water averaged 136 mg/L. Hard water leaves scale on the inside of pipes and heaters, which is one reason a softener or filter often comes up during a repipe.

The same report says lead and copper at the tap come mainly from corrosion of household plumbing, and the City cannot control the materials in your home. It advises flushing the tap 30 seconds to 2 minutes when water has sat for several hours. If your home has old pipes, replacing them is one way to address that. Ask the City if you have questions about your tap water.
